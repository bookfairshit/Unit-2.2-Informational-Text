Rhetoric Remix — Same Crisis, Different Audience

Objective: Explain how audience and purpose change the rhetorical choices a speaker makes; support the explanation with exact language from a student-created text.

Standards

ELA.9.R.2.3 primary: how appeals establish and achieve a specific, stated purpose; whether ethos, logos and pathos fit.

ELA.9.R.3.4 supporting: the rhetoric’s contribution, not device spotting.

Success criterion

1 cited choice + 1 plausible audience effect + 1 “why” explanation.

Deliverable

Two versions + audience-effect comparison.

MinWhat happens
0–5Bellwork (composition notebook)“Would you rather hear a leader say ‘Everything is fine’ or ‘Things are difficult, but here is our plan’? Why?” Two sentences; one quick share.
5–11Retrieval / completionProject one short FDR selection from Lumio. Call for green ethos, yellow logos, red pathos. Ask: What does this language attempt to make listeners believe or feel? If the Lumio task is unfinished, use this time for completion.
11–15Mini-modelTeacher-written neutral scenario (teacher card on the scenario page): “A school event is postponed because of severe weather. A new date will be announced tomorrow.” Model how an announcement to anxious students versus families might differ. Do not pretend every appeal is present.
15–28Creative task (groups of 3–4)Each group gets one scenario card and two audience cards. Write two 2–3 sentence announcements on the group remix sheet. Mark one purposeful appeal in each with green, yellow or red and label its effect. Rotate a speaker to deliver each version in a contrasting tone.
28–36Kinesthetic “Audience Switch”On cue, groups swap one audience card with a neighboring group, revise one sentence in 90 seconds, then stand and read the revision. Listeners vote on a slip which version better fits the new audience and must justify with a word or phrase.
36–42Individual transfer (spiral notebook)Entry title 11/12 — Rhetoric Remix: Audience Matters. Copy one original line and the revised line, name one rhetorical choice, and write: “Changing ___ to ___ makes the audience more likely to ___ because ___.”
42–45Exit ticket (slip or Lumio)Independently explain which FDR rhetorical choice was best suited to the audience in 1933 and why; cite one brief phrase. Collect and sort to group students for the next teacher table.

Sample teacher model (edit freely)

To anxious students: “We know you were looking forward to this, and so were we. Your safety matters more than any event, so we are postponing it. Watch for the new date tomorrow.”
Red (pathos): “so were we” shares the disappointment, so students feel understood instead of brushed off.

To families: “Because severe weather is expected, the event is postponed so no one has to travel in unsafe conditions. We will announce the new date tomorrow.”
Yellow (logos): “Because severe weather is expected” gives a reason, so families see the decision as sensible.

Each version carries one clear appeal. Say so out loud: not every message uses all three.

Differentiation

  • Sentence frames (on the spiral frame half sheet)
  • Preprinted neutral scenario
  • Optional oral rehearsal before writing
  • Extension: When might an emotional appeal be manipulative, or not enough without evidence?

Teacher talk

“A rhetorical device is not a scavenger-hunt prize. The question is what the choice does to a particular audience.”

Materials

Scenario cards, two audience cards per group, colored pencils or highlighters, FDR excerpts, timer, notebooks.

FDR launch → MLK bridge · school day 4 of 24 · Proposed

Museum of Persuasion — From Fear to Dream

Objective: Analyze how rhetorical choices respond to a specific historical need and predict how a later speaker might use rhetoric for a different purpose.

Standards

ELA.9.R.3.4 primary: explain the author’s use of rhetoric and its contribution to the text.

ELA.9.R.2.3 supporting: appeals and a specific, stated purpose.

Success criterion

The placard identifies a choice, cites a phrase, and convincingly explains its effect; the individual exit compares purposes.

Deliverable

Evidence-based gallery label and exit.

MinWhat happens
0–5Bellwork (composition notebook)“What makes a speech worthy of being remembered?” Write one quality and a specific example.
5–10Context bridgeProject the two-column bridge (board text page). Economic crisis in 1933; civil rights struggle in 1963. Fill the rows with the class in a few words each. No long history lecture: the point is that circumstances and audiences differ.
10–14ModelMake a 3-line museum label: excerpt / rhetorical choice / effect on the intended audience. Say the causal link out loud (“so listeners…”), not just the label.
14–30Gallery constructionGroups receive one of three FDR excerpt cards and a half-sheet placard. Required: artifact title; 8–15 word excerpt; 1933 context in one sentence; device and evidence; effect on listeners; one question to visitors. Students mount and display the placard.
30–39Museum walkGroups circulate. At each display visitors leave a sticky note that answers the question or makes a specific challenge to the analysis. An optional docent explains the label; rotate roles so no one only watches.
39–45MLK teaserPlay a short, appropriately sourced MLK audio/video selection or read a brief licensed excerpt (captions or transcript on screen). Students complete the frame: “FDR needed Americans to ___. MLK needed Americans to ___. A rhetorical choice both might use is ___, but its effect differs because ___.”
45–50Individual exit (spiral notebook)Entry title 11/13 — Museum of Persuasion. Choose one FDR line and one MLK teaser line or idea; explain a similarity or difference in purpose using audience and context.

Model label (edit freely)

Model with Thursday’s teacher-written announcement so you do not use up a group’s FDR excerpt:

Excerpt: “We know you were looking forward to this, and so were we.”

Choice: Pathos: shared disappointment (“so were we”).

Effect: Anxious students hear that the adults feel it too, so they are more likely to accept the postponement instead of blaming the school.

Or model with an FDR line that is not on any group’s card.

Access

Display captions or transcripts, avoid requiring public speaking from every student, and provide printed evidence.

Fallback

If Thursday runs long, cut the gallery to two displays and use Friday to finish FDR annotations before the MLK teaser.

Materials

3 FDR excerpt cards, placard template, tape, sticky notes, timer, MLK clip with transcript, projector.

Needs before class: the team’s selected FDR excerpts (copy onto the excerpt cards) and an MLK clip or licensed excerpt with captions or a transcript. Neither is reproduced in this pack.

Fri Nov 13 · Gallery roles + sticky-note startersCut on dashed lines · rotate roles at each stop

Gallery roles

Role · optional

Docent

Stay with your placard for one rotation. In 30 seconds, explain the excerpt, the choice and its effect. Then hand the role to a teammate.

Role

Visitor: answer

Answer the placard’s question on a sticky note. Use a word or phrase from the excerpt.

Role

Visitor: challenge

Leave a sticky note that pushes on the analysis: a different effect, a missing phrase, or a better-fitting appeal.

Role

Recorder

After the walk, read your group’s sticky notes and pick one to answer or use in a revision.

Sticky-note starter · answer

The phrase “____” suggests ____ because ____.

Sticky-note starter · challenge

The label says ____, but “____” could also make listeners ____.

Sticky-note starter · challenge

I would add the phrase “____” as evidence because ____.

Sticky-note starter · question

Would a 1933 listener who ____ react the same way? Why?

A specific challenge beats “nice job.” Every sticky note quotes or points to the excerpt.Fri Nov 13
