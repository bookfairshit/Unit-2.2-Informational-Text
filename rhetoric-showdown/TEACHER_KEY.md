## The Big Three · Ethos • Pathos • Logos

### APPEALS-200 · 200

**Clue:** Which rhetorical appeal tries to persuade an audience through **emotion**?

**Answer:** Pathos.

**Acceptable:** Emotional appeal.

**Why it works:** Pathos seeks to move feelings such as hope, fear, outrage, compassion, or pride.

**Teacher note:** Ask for one feeling an audience might experience.

*Source: Definition / class terminology.*

### APPEALS-400 · 400

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “I have spent twenty years working as a nurse, and I have seen firsthand what happens when hospitals are understaffed.” Which appeal is strongest?

**Answer:** Ethos.

**Acceptable:** Credibility / authority / firsthand experience.

**Why it works:** The speaker establishes expertise and personal experience to build trust.

**Teacher note:** Experience could also be evidence, but the rhetorical emphasis is the speaker’s credibility.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### APPEALS-600 · 600

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “The library served 1,200 students last month, but its hours have been cut by 40%.” Identify the dominant appeal **and** the evidence that creates it.

**Answer:** Logos; the numerical evidence (1,200 students and/or a 40% reduction).

**Acceptable:** Logical appeal using statistics/data.

**Why it works:** The figures invite a reason-based judgment about a mismatch between demand and access.

**Teacher note:** Require BOTH logos and evidence to receive full credit.

*Source: Created practice example; figures are fictional.*

### APPEALS-800 · 800

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “Imagine the students who lose their only quiet place to study when the library closes early.” Identify the dominant appeal **and** its intended effect on the audience.

**Answer:** Pathos; it creates sympathy/concern for students and motivates support for keeping the library open.

**Acceptable:** A defensible emotional response plus a plausible intended action.

**Why it works:** Readers picture a personal cost, making a policy change feel urgent.

**Teacher note:** Naming “pathos” alone is insufficient.

### APPEALS-1000 · 1000

**Answer:** Ethos = principal role / credibility; logos = attendance data and cause-and-effect pattern.

**Acceptable:** Same two appeals with clearly justified textual evidence.

**Teacher note:** Require BOTH appeals AND what makes each effective.

## Spot the Device

### DEVICES-200 · 200

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “We will learn. We will lead. We will change.” What is the repeated phrase at the **beginning** of successive clauses called?

**Answer:** Anaphora.

**Acceptable:** Repetition at the beginnings of clauses; accept “repetition” if student explains positioning, not otherwise.

**Why it works:** Beginning each clause with “We will” creates rhythm and emphasis.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### DEVICES-400 · 400

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “A classroom without books is a garden without sunlight.” Name the figurative device.

**Answer:** Metaphor.

**Acceptable:** Analogy / metaphorical comparison with explanation (teacher discretion).

**Why it works:** It directly presents the classroom as something that cannot flourish without a necessary resource; it does not use “like” or “as.”

*Source: Created practice example.*

### DEVICES-600 · 600

**Clue:** A speaker asks, “What kind of future do we want for our children?” but does **not** expect the audience to respond aloud. Name the device and explain why the speaker uses it.

**Answer:** Rhetorical question; prompts reflection and invites the audience to consider responsibility for the future.

**Acceptable:** Question intended to make a point / provoke thought, plus a logical effect.

**Why it works:** A rhetorical question engages thinking without requesting a literal reply.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### DEVICES-800 · 800

**Clue (MLK VERBATIM EXCERPT):** King describes a “bad check” returned with “insufficient funds.” What broader idea does this **extended financial metaphor** communicate?

**Answer:** The United States promised equal rights/freedom but failed to deliver that promise to Black Americans.

**Acceptable:** Unfulfilled promise of equality; America has not honored its founding ideals.

**Why it works:** The financial metaphor makes broken national promises concrete and understandable.

### DEVICES-1000 · 1000

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “Our city needs courage, not complacency; action, not excuses.” Identify the key device **and** explain how the contrast strengthens persuasion.

**Answer:** Antithesis / parallel contrast; it puts the desired actions against their opposites, making the choice seem clear and urgent.

**Acceptable:** Juxtaposition/contrast with specific explanation; parallelism with explanation of paired opposites.

**Why it works:** Balanced opposing terms create memorable, forceful alternatives.

**Teacher note:** Accept “contrast” if explanation is precise; don’t demand an unfamiliar Greek-derived term.

*Source: Created practice example.*

## Purpose · P.I.E. & Beyond

### PURPOSE-200 · 200

**Clue:** In the author’s purpose mnemonic **P.I.E.**, what do the three letters stand for?

**Answer:** Persuade, Inform, Entertain.

**Acceptable:** All three meanings, any order if clearly matched.

**Why it works:** P.I.E. is a starting point for identifying why a writer or speaker creates a text.

*Source: Class mnemonic.*

### PURPOSE-400 · 400

**Answer:** Inform.

**Acceptable:** Provide factual details / announce a meeting.

**Why it works:** This sentence mainly shares logistical information without trying to change opinions.

*Source: Created example; details are fictional.*

### PURPOSE-600 · 600

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “Protect our parks. Sign the petition before Friday.” Identify the primary purpose **and** the action the writer wants.

**Answer:** Persuade; motivate readers to sign the petition to protect parks.

**Acceptable:** Persuasion/call to action with an explicit signing action.

**Why it works:** An imperative and deadline aim to change audience behavior.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### PURPOSE-800 · 800

**Clue:** FDR’s 1933 inaugural address gives information about a national crisis, yet its **primary** purpose is persuasion. Explain **what he wants Americans to believe or do**.

**Answer:** Trust leadership and support collective, decisive action to confront the Great Depression rather than yield to fear.

**Acceptable:** Build confidence, national resolve, and support for recovery action, with reasoning.

**Why it works:** A speech can contain information while primarily attempting to change attitudes and actions.

*Source: Historical speech/context.*

### PURPOSE-1000 · 1000

**Clue:** A speech can make an audience feel hopeful **and** call them to change an unjust system. Does the emotion make its primary purpose “entertain”? Explain using MLK’s “I Have a Dream.”

**Answer:** No. MLK uses hope and emotion as strategies to persuade listeners to support civil rights, equality, and change; creating emotion does not equal entertainment.

**Acceptable:** Clear distinction between emotional *method* and persuasive *purpose* supported with MLK context.

**Why it works:** Purpose is the speaker’s desired outcome; rhetorical appeals are strategies used to achieve it.

**Teacher note:** An answer of merely “persuade” is insufficient for this value.

*Source: Historical speech/context.*

**Answer:** Martin Luther King Jr.; civil rights/racial equality for Black Americans.

**Answer:** Anaphora (also repetition).

**Why it works:** Repetition of a phrase at the start of clauses builds rhythm and reinforces a vision of equality.

**Acceptable:** Ethos based on respected national ideals; appeals to shared values/credible founding documents.

**Why it works:** King frames equal rights as honoring American promises, not abandoning them.

**Why it works:** The metaphor blends understandable logic and moral urgency.

### MLK-1000 · 1000

## Rhetoric Lab · Name It & Prove It

### LAB-200 · 200

**Answer:** Call to action (an imperative), not supporting evidence.

**Acceptable:** Request urging action.

**Why it works:** The sentence directs behavior but supplies no proof for why someone should join.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### LAB-400 · 400

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “No student should be denied a meal, because everyone deserves the chance to learn.” Identify the **claim**.

**Answer:** No student should be denied a meal.

**Acceptable:** Every student deserves access to food/school meals.

**Why it works:** The first idea is the position; the second gives the reason.

*Source: Created practice example.*

### LAB-600 · 600

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “Ninety percent of students in our survey said they prefer a longer lunch.” Give **one question** a careful reader should ask before trusting this statistic.

**Answer:** How many were surveyed? Who was surveyed? Was the sample representative? How was the question worded? Who conducted the survey?

**Acceptable:** Any sensible question about sample size, bias, method, or evidence quality.

**Why it works:** A persuasive statistic can be misleading if the sample or method is weak.

*Source: Created example; statistic is fictional.*

### LAB-800 · 800

**Clue (CREATED EXAMPLE):** “Our tired hallways are begging for a fresh coat of paint.” Identify the figurative device **and** its effect.

**Answer:** Personification; it makes the building seem neglected or urgently in need of care, encouraging support for improvement.

**Acceptable:** Giving human traits to hallways plus a plausible effect on the audience.

**Why it works:** “Begging” assigns a human action to a nonhuman place.

*Source: Created practice example.*

## Final Round · The Power of Rhetoric

**Wager directions:** Choose a wager. Write only the wager on the board and hold it up when asked. Mr. Moody records it before showing the final clue.

**Clue:** FDR tried to move Americans from **fear toward action**. MLK tried to move Americans from **injustice toward equality**. Name **one rhetorical strategy used by each speaker** and explain **how each strategy supports that speaker’s purpose**.

**Model answer:** FDR uses reassuring, forceful diction and the memorable claim about fearing fear itself to calm panic and build support for national action. MLK uses the repeated “I have a dream” refrain and/or the broken-promise metaphor to build hope, emphasize the injustice of unmet promises, and rally support for equal rights.

**Acceptable:** One accurate strategy for FDR + linked effect AND one accurate strategy for MLK + linked effect. Examples: FDR’s authoritative ethos, unity-building language, confident tone; MLK’s anaphora, extended metaphor, pathos, references to founding promises. The answer must link each strategy to a concrete persuasive goal.

**Tie breaker (optional, no automatic score change):** What is the difference between an author’s *purpose* and a rhetorical *strategy*? **Model:** Purpose is what the author wants the audience to think/do; strategy is the technique used to influence that outcome.
