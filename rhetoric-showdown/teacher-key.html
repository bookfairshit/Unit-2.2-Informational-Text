The Big Three Ethos • Pathos • Logos

200 APPEALS-200

Which rhetorical appeal tries to persuade an audience through emotion?

Answer: Pathos.

Acceptable: Emotional appeal.

Why it works: Pathos seeks to move feelings such as hope, fear, outrage, compassion, or pride.

Teacher note: Ask for one feeling an audience might experience.

Source: Definition / class terminology.

400 APPEALS-400 · CREATED EXAMPLE

“I have spent twenty years working as a nurse, and I have seen firsthand what happens when hospitals are understaffed.” Which appeal is strongest?

Answer: Ethos.

Acceptable: Credibility / authority / firsthand experience.

Why it works: The speaker establishes expertise and personal experience to build trust.

Teacher note: Experience could also be evidence, but the rhetorical emphasis is the speaker’s credibility.

Source: Created practice example.

600 APPEALS-600 · CREATED EXAMPLE

“The library served 1,200 students last month, but its hours have been cut by 40%.” Identify the dominant appeal and the evidence that creates it.

Answer: Logos; the numerical evidence (1,200 students and/or a 40% reduction).

Acceptable: Logical appeal using statistics/data.

Why it works: The figures invite a reason-based judgment about a mismatch between demand and access.

Teacher note: Require BOTH logos and evidence to receive full credit.

Spot the Device

200 DEVICES-200 · CREATED EXAMPLE

“We will learn. We will lead. We will change.” What is the repeated phrase at the beginning of successive clauses called?

Answer: Anaphora.

Acceptable: Repetition at the beginnings of clauses; accept “repetition” if student explains positioning, not otherwise.

Why it works: Beginning each clause with “We will” creates rhythm and emphasis.

Source: Created practice example.

400 DEVICES-400 · CREATED EXAMPLE

Answer: Metaphor.

Acceptable: Analogy / metaphorical comparison with explanation (teacher discretion).

Why it works: It directly presents the classroom as something that cannot flourish without a necessary resource; it does not use “like” or “as.”

1000 DEVICES-1000 · CREATED EXAMPLE

“Our city needs courage, not complacency; action, not excuses.” Identify the key device and explain how the contrast strengthens persuasion.

Answer: Antithesis / parallel contrast; it puts the desired actions against their opposites, making the choice seem clear and urgent.

Acceptable: Juxtaposition/contrast with specific explanation; parallelism with explanation of paired opposites.

Why it works: Balanced opposing terms create memorable, forceful alternatives.

Teacher note: Accept “contrast” if explanation is precise; don’t demand an unfamiliar Greek-derived term.

Purpose P.I.E. & Beyond

200 PURPOSE-200

In the author’s purpose mnemonic P.I.E., what do the three letters stand for?

Answer: Persuade, Inform, Entertain.

Acceptable: All three meanings, any order if clearly matched.

Why it works: P.I.E. is a starting point for identifying why a writer or speaker creates a text.

Source: Class mnemonic.

Answer: Persuade; motivate readers to sign the petition to protect parks.

800 PURPOSE-800

FDR’s 1933 inaugural address gives information about a national crisis, yet its primary purpose is persuasion. Explain what he wants Americans to believe or do.

Answer: Trust leadership and support collective, decisive action to confront the Great Depression rather than yield to fear.

Acceptable: Build confidence, national resolve, and support for recovery action, with reasoning.

Why it works: A speech can contain information while primarily attempting to change attitudes and actions.

Source: Historical speech/context.

1000 PURPOSE-1000

A speech can make an audience feel hopeful and call them to change an unjust system. Does the emotion make its primary purpose “entertain”? Explain using MLK’s “I Have a Dream.”

Answer: No. MLK uses hope and emotion as strategies to persuade listeners to support civil rights, equality, and change; creating emotion does not equal entertainment.

Acceptable: Clear distinction between emotional method and persuasive purpose supported with MLK context.

Why it works: Purpose is the speaker’s desired outcome; rhetorical appeals are strategies used to achieve it.

Teacher note: An answer of merely “persuade” is insufficient for this value.

Source: Historical speech/context.

Who delivered “I Have a Dream,” and what major cause was he advocating?

Acceptable: MLK / Dr. King and equality / ending racial segregation and discrimination.

Why it works: The 1963 speech urged fulfillment of promises of freedom and equal rights.

King repeatedly says, “I have a dream.” Identify the rhetorical device that this repetition exemplifies.

Answer: Anaphora (also repetition).

Acceptable: Repetition; anaphora.

Why it works: Repetition of a phrase at the start of clauses builds rhythm and reinforces a vision of equality.

Source: Short quotation from MLK’s speech.

600 MLK-600

King refers to the Constitution and Declaration of Independence when discussing America’s promise of equality. Why are these documents persuasive to his audience?

Answer: They invoke the nation’s founding principles/shared commitments, lending historical and moral authority to the demand for equal rights.

Acceptable: Ethos based on respected national ideals; appeals to shared values/credible founding documents.

Why it works: King frames equal rights as honoring American promises, not abandoning them.

Teacher note: Accept an evidence-based logos interpretation if students explicitly connect constitutional promises to his claim; dominant instructional reading is appeal to shared credibility/values.

Source: Speech content, paraphrased.

800 MLK-800

King’s “promissory note” and “bad check” comparison appears early in the speech. Explain how this comparison helps him advance his purpose.

Answer: It turns the abstract claim of broken equality promises into a familiar unpaid debt, showing that justice is overdue and encouraging the audience to demand that the promise be honored.

Acceptable: A precise connection between the metaphor, promised-but-denied equality, and urgent change.

Why it works: The metaphor blends understandable logic and moral urgency.

Source: Speech content, paraphrased.

1000 MLK-1000

In his dream passages, King shifts from describing injustice toward imagining a more equal future. What does that shift accomplish for his audience? Give two effects.

Answer: It offers hope/a positive vision, builds unity and shared aspiration, and/or motivates continued nonviolent action for equality. TWO distinct effects required.

Acceptable: Any two defensible effects grounded in the dream sequence.

Why it works: The movement from injustice to possibility makes the struggle feel both necessary and achievable.

Teacher note: If students name an appeal, require a concrete audience effect too.

Source: Speech structure, paraphrased.

Final Round The Power of Rhetoric

Wager directions: Choose a wager. Write only the wager on the board and hold it up when asked. Mr. Moody records it before showing the final clue.

FDR tried to move Americans from fear toward action. MLK tried to move Americans from injustice toward equality. Name one rhetorical strategy used by each speaker and explain how each strategy supports that speaker’s purpose.

Model answer: FDR uses reassuring, forceful diction and the memorable claim about fearing fear itself to calm panic and build support for national action. MLK uses the repeated “I have a dream” refrain and/or the broken-promise metaphor to build hope, emphasize the injustice of unmet promises, and rally support for equal rights.

Acceptable: One accurate strategy for FDR + linked effect AND one accurate strategy for MLK + linked effect. Examples: FDR’s authoritative ethos, unity-building language, confident tone; MLK’s anaphora, extended metaphor, pathos, references to founding promises. The answer must link each strategy to a concrete persuasive goal.

Tie breaker: What is the difference between an author’s purpose and a rhetorical strategy? Model: Purpose is what the author wants the audience to think/do; strategy is the technique used to influence that outcome.
