Museum of Persuasion

Lumio version: pairs with museum-of-persuasion-nov-13.pptx. 50-minute model; adapt to your bell. A proposed plan, not a record of implemented instruction.

Before class

  • Add your MLK clip (captioned, appropriately sourced) to slide 15.
  • Check that every table has a device that can open its exhibit slide.
  • Sticky notes on each table, or tell visitors to type notes in Lumio.
  • Timer visible.
  • Know which plan you ran Thursday (see the last slide if the remix did not finish).

Goal

Students analyze how FDR’s rhetorical choices answered what 1933 needed, then predict how a later speaker might use rhetoric for a different purpose. Standards: ELA.9.R.3.4 (primary), ELA.9.R.2.3 (supporting). Success: each label identifies a choice, cites a phrase and convincingly explains its effect; the exit entry compares purposes.

0–5 min

Bellwork

Slide 2
  1. Do

    Slide 2 is up as students walk in. Start a 3-minute timer.

  2. Say

    Composition notebooks out. Here's today's question: What makes a speech worth remembering? Give me one quality and one specific example. It can be a speech, a song lyric, a coach's pep talk, a line from a movie. Three minutes.

  3. Do

    Circulate. Look for answers that name what the words did to the people listening, not just a topic.

  4. Ask

    Take two shares: "Who wrote a quality about what the words did to the people hearing them?"

  5. Say

    That's our job today. We're not hunting for devices. We're figuring out why certain words were worth remembering for the people who heard them.

5–10 min

Context bridge

Slide 3
  1. Say

    Yesterday we listened to FDR in 1933. Today we turn his words into a museum, and at the end of class we'll get a first taste of a speech from thirty years later. First, just enough context. Five minutes, not a history lecture.

  2. Ask

    Point to the 1933 column. "Who was listening to FDR? What were they facing? What did he need them to do?" Type short answers into the boxes as students give them.

  3. Listen for

    People across the country, many listening on the radio; people who had lost jobs or savings; banks failing; fear. He needed them to stop panicking, trust the new government and back quick action.

  4. Say

    Point to the 1963 column. "In August 1963, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. spoke at the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom."

  5. Ask

    "Who might have been listening? What were they facing?" Leave the last box empty: "Hold the question of what he needed them to do. We'll come back to it at the end."

  6. Say

    This is yesterday's remix in real history: a different crisis and a different audience, so different choices.

10–14 min

Model label

Slide 4
  1. Say

    Every exhibit in a museum has a label. Ours has three lines: the excerpt, the choice and the effect. Watch me build one with yesterday's fireworks message, so I don't use up anyone's FDR line.

  2. Say

    Think aloud: "Excerpt: 'We know you were counting down to the fireworks, and so were we.' Choice: pathos, a shared disappointment, right here in 'and so were we.' Effect: kids hear that the grown-ups feel it too, so they're more likely to accept the delay instead of being mad at the city."

  3. Say

    Did you hear the word "so"? That's the causal link. If your effect line has no "so" or "because," you've only named a device. A device is not a scavenger-hunt prize.

  4. Check

    "Thumbs up if you could point to the exact words that did the work. Sideways if you'd want help finding them." Note the sideways tables and visit them first.

14–30 min

Gallery construction

Slides 5–13
  1. Do

    Show slide 5. Send each table to its exhibit slide in Lumio (Table 1 = slide 6 ... Table 8 = slide 13). Start a 16-minute timer.

  2. Say

    Every table is now a team of curators. Your exhibit is one line from FDR's speech. Open your exhibit slide and fill in all six parts of the label. Two rules: your excerpt is 8 to 15 exact words, and your effect line needs a "so" or a "because."

  3. Say

    Tables 3 and 7 have the same line, and so do Tables 6 and 8. Visitors are going to compare you, so make your reading your own.

  4. Check

    At 5 minutes: "You should have a title and your excerpt." At 10: "Choice and evidence done. Now the effect." At 14: "Write your visitor question. Make it one people could actually disagree about."

  5. Ask

    While circulating: "Which exact word does the work?" · "Who in 1933 is this line for?" · "Read me your effect. Where's your 'so'?" · "Could this be a different color? Defend it."

  6. If

    A group writes "It's pathos because it's emotional": ask "Which emotion, in whom, and what does that feeling make them do?"

  7. If

    The line is too long for 8–15 words (Exhibits 3 and 7): "Pick the words that carry the punch, and be ready to say why you cut there."

30–39 min

Museum walk

Slide 14
  1. Do

    Each table leaves its exhibit open on a device at the table. Short on devices? Project the exhibits one at a time and walk the class through them.

  2. Say

    Choose one docent to stay with your exhibit. Everyone else is a visitor. You'll visit three exhibits, and at least one has to be a different line from yours. At each stop, leave a sticky note that answers their question or challenges their analysis. "Nice job" is not a sticky note. Quote or point to the excerpt.

  3. Do

    Rotate every 3 minutes. Each time, say: "Rotate, and switch docents," so no one spends the whole walk watching.

  4. Do

    Sticky notes can be paper, or visitors can type a note right on the exhibit slide in Lumio.

  5. Say

    At the last rotation: "Docents, read your sticky notes. Pick one you'd answer or use to revise your label."

39–45 min

MLK teaser

Slide 15
  1. Do

    Play the MLK clip you chose, with captions on. Add it to slide 15 before class.

  2. Say

    Before the clip: "Thirty years after FDR, the country was facing a different crisis. As you listen to Dr. King, ask yourself one thing: what did he need his listeners to do?"

  3. Say

    After the clip: "Back to our bridge. Let's fill in the 1963 box we left empty. What did he need his listeners to do?"

  4. Say

    Now finish the frame on the slide: FDR needed Americans to ___. MLK needed Americans to ___. A rhetorical choice both might use is ___, but its effect differs because ___.

  5. Ask

    Take one or two. Push for audience and context: "Who was each one talking to?"

45–50 min

Exit · spiral notebook

Slide 16
  1. Say

    Spiral notebooks. Title it "11/13 — Museum of Persuasion." Choose one FDR line and one line or idea from the King clip. In three or four sentences, explain one similarity or difference in their purpose, using audience and context.

  2. Say

    Not "they both use pathos." Tell me who each one was talking to and what each one needed.

  3. Say

    To close: "Monday we start Dr. King's speech for real. Bring today's question with you: what did he need his listeners to do?"

  4. Do

    Collect the entries or snap photos of them to plan Monday's teacher table. Group by what you see, not by assumed level.

If the day goes sideways

  • Thursday's table remix didn't happen (you played the full speech)? Run the remix first today from the Nov 12 deck and open the museum on Monday. Update the team calendar.
  • Short on time: cut the museum walk to two rotations and skip docents. Protect the MLK teaser and the exit entry.
  • Thursday ran long and FDR annotations aren't done: cut the gallery to two displays and use the time to finish the annotations before the MLK teaser.
  • Not enough devices: project each exhibit and run the walk as a whole class, with tables writing one sticky note per exhibit.
