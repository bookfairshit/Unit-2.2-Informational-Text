The Toolbox

Examples marked practice were written for this lesson. They are not King’s words.

A · Language that creates an image

TermWhat it isPractice exampleWhy it fits
SimileA comparison of unlike things using like or as.“The unanswered request hung over the room like a storm cloud.”Suggests tension or worry.
MetaphorPresents one thing as another without like or as.“The unanswered request was a storm cloud over the meeting.”Not literally weather.
HyperboleDeliberate exaggeration for emphasis; not meant literally.“I have asked for this change a million times.”Overstatement shows frustration.
PersonificationHuman actions or qualities given to something nonhuman.“The warning sign begged us to slow down.”Makes the warning feel urgent.

Mini-model: “The room felt like a sealed jar.” Identify the comparison (room → sealed jar), explain its meaning (cramped, airless), explain its effect (an uneasy, trapped mood). A label alone leaves the analysis unfinished. Counterexample: “I like this proposal” is not a simile; it states a preference.

B · Language that shapes an argument

TermWhat it isPractice exampleWhat it can do
AllusionA reference to a recognizable text, event, person, or idea outside the passage.“Our team is facing its own David and Goliath moment.” (In the story, a smaller challenger confronts a powerful opponent.)Frames the team as an underdog; encourages courage or hope.
AnaphoraRepeating words at the beginnings of successive clauses or sentences.“We need safe streets. We need open doors. We need action now.”Emphasizes shared needs; builds momentum.
AntithesisContrasting ideas in a balanced structure.“Choose cooperation over conflict; choose action over excuses.”Frames a choice; stresses the preferred one.

Also: parallelism uses similar grammatical patterns to organize connected ideas (the opening words need not match). A rhetorical question is asked mainly to provoke thought or emphasize a point.

C · Appeals explain a route to persuasion

AppealWhat it isPractice example and how it works
EthosCredibility, character, or shared ethical authority.“As the nurse who treats these injuries each week, I ask you to make this crossing safer.” Relevant experience gives listeners a reason to trust her judgment.
LogosReasons, evidence, or a logical connection.“The crossing has no signal, and drivers cannot see students behind parked cars; adding a signal would address that danger.” Connects a problem with a relevant solution.
PathosFeelings that matter to the audience and the issue.“No child should be afraid on the walk to school.” Encourages concern for children’s safety.

Devices and appeals overlap. Ask: why is this appeal a reasonable fit for this audience and goal? “It uses pathos” is a starting point, not an explanation. Mood is the emotional atmosphere for listeners; tone is the speaker’s attitude.

Optional practice

If you have time before A, B, and C: write the question number and your answer letter in your spiral, with one reason. All practice sentences were written for this lesson; none are King’s words.

T1. Which sentence uses a simile?
  • A. “I like the new schedule.”
  • B. “The final minute crawled past like a sleepy turtle.”
  • C. “Our school is beside the library.”
T2. “This backpack weighs ten thousand pounds.” In an ordinary school conversation, what makes this hyperbole?
  • A. It deliberately exaggerates the weight to emphasize how heavy it feels.
  • B. It proves the speaker measured the backpack accurately.
  • C. Any sentence about a heavy object is hyperbole.
T3. “We ask for fairness. We ask for safety. We ask for change.” What makes this anaphora?
  • A. The sentences all have a serious subject.
  • B. The same opening words begin successive sentences.
  • C. The speaker asks an actual question.
T4. A speaker mentions a bus driver’s twenty years of safely transporting children. Why might that help persuade parents to consider the driver’s safety recommendation?
  • A. The driver’s relevant experience gives parents a reason to trust the recommendation.
  • B. Mentioning a job makes every recommendation correct.
  • C. The detail guarantees that all parents will agree.
I1. “For families waiting for help, the office was a maze with no exit.” Which explanation best accounts for the metaphor’s effect?
  • A. It gives exact information about the building’s floor plan.
  • B. It makes the process seem confusing and inescapable, creating a frustrated or helpless atmosphere.
  • C. It proves all offices are physically dangerous.
I3. “A fair decision could open a window in a room that had felt sealed shut.” Write one sentence: what does the window image mean, and what mood does it create?
R1. “Now we can listen. Now we can plan. Now we can act.” What do the repeated openings accomplish?
  • A. They organize a movement from listening to action and emphasize that the audience can begin immediately.
  • B. They show the speaker ran out of vocabulary.
  • C. They establish that all three activities already happened in the past.
R3. “Our goal is not louder arguments but safer streets.” In one sentence, explain why a concern for safety could be an appropriate appeal for an audience of neighborhood families.

practice passage “The neighborhood should add a marked crossing near the library. Students currently cross between parked cars, where drivers have trouble seeing them. At Tuesday’s meeting, three families described near misses at that location. The library’s front door is painted blue.”

E1. Which two details most directly support adding the crossing?
  • A. The sightline problem and families’ reports of near misses.
  • B. The color of the door and the day of the meeting.
  • C. The library’s existence and the fact that families attend meetings.
E2. A student says, “The three reports are useful, so they prove exactly how often near misses happen all year.” In 1–2 sentences, explain what is reasonable about that statement and what goes beyond the evidence.

A. Language → Mood → Purpose

Choose one example of figurative language. Give the evidence, explain what the comparison or image means, and explain how it creates a particular mood. Connect that effect to a specific change King wants his audience to support.

Optional starter: “The image of ___ presents ___ as ___. This creates a ___ mood because ___. That helps King encourage his audience to ___.”

B. Rhetoric → Appeal → Audience

Choose a different example of a rhetorical device, such as repeated openings, a contrast, or an allusion. Explain what the device emphasizes or helps listeners understand. Explain how it supports an appeal and why that appeal fits King’s audience and purpose.

Optional starter: “By ___, King emphasizes ___. This appeals to ___ through ___, which fits his audience and purpose because ___.”

C. Support → Judgment

State one central idea developed in these portions of the speech. Compare two specific supports from different places in the assigned reading. Which support is more convincing for that idea, or how do they strengthen the idea together? Explain your judgment.

Optional starter: “King develops the idea that ___. The detail about ___ supports it by ___, while the detail about ___ adds ___. [One is stronger / Together they are convincing] because ___.”

You may reuse evidence from A or B here, but judge its value as support. Use different evidence in A and B.
